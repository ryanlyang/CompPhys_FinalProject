Additional Comments
1. Start with a precise physics question that you wish to answer using a computer. Writing “I plan to study X” is too vague. Instead, write something like this: “Would the gravitational influence of planet 9, as proposed by Prof. Brown at Caltech, alter the orbit of Earth to sufficiently change the pacing of the ice ages? To answer this question, I will compare the simulated orbits, computed by integrating Newton’s equations of motion for the planets, to Earth’s geological record.” If you start with a precise and interesting question, all else will follow naturally. You must not recycle a project from another class; do something new.
2. Some students think they must read and understand everything before beginning work. This is a mistake. Instead, begin by writing a simple simulation right after creating a precise scientific question. Correct and refine the simulation as you learn more.
3. Citations must be made throughout the paper; it is not enough to simply include a bibliography at the end. Instead, items in the bibliography must refer to key ideas in the body of your report. For examples, see any paper in Physical Review journals. Your references must include at least the following information: authors, full title, journal name, volume number, pages, and year of publication. Textbooks need to include the publisher and location of the publisher. It is recommended to use LaTex/BibTeX to manage your citations.
Read about plagiarism to make sure you understand what it is and how to avoid making a seirous mistake. TurnItIn software will be used to check for plagiarism. For more help, you can consult with the physical sciences librarian for help with citation or other resources. Consult with the Writing Center for help with writing.
4. Any figures or images you did not produce must cite the source reference. This can be placed in the figure caption.
5. It is highly recommended to use LaTeX and BibTeX to write up the paper. Overleaf is a popular browser-based way to write LaTeX. It is also recommended to use revtex 4-2, the style file for the Physical Review Journals, to style your LaTeX file.
6. A good project report will loosely follow the following sections. The score that you receive on the final project will be based on both content, and on the presentation.
i) Title and abstract: The abstract should be a brief summary or elevator-pitch of your paper
ii) Introduction: What is the question you are trying to study? Why is it interesting? What are the key questions you are trying to answer. Include background information as needed. This should not be dragged down by technical details; stay with the big picture.
iii) Background and theory: Include specific details on background information to bring the reader up to speed. This may include, e.g. any important mathematical equations.
iv) Method of solution or investigation: Discuss how you solve the problem, e.g. algorithms and calculational details. You must address the reliability of your work. You must include control experiments that test, for example, the conservation of energy or other known properties.
v) Results: Present your results in a compact, concise, and appealing way. Discuss errors and reliability of your results.
vi) Discussion: How do these results compare to the literature?
vii) Conclusions: Zooming back out, what was the main result of your work? What was the point? How can it be improved upon in future work?
viii) References
xi) Appendices: Include technical details here that would have interfered with the flow of the original paper.
